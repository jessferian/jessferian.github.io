CASE STUDY: Automation Rules. Giving dealers control over where their cars are published, without needing support.
Role: Product Designer, owned the rule builder UX
Team: Product Manager, tech lead, engineering, co-designed with Eagers
Shipped: V2.0 national release, February 2026
Key numbers: 102 → 228 (dealers using rules (+124%)); 344 (rules created); 1,300+ hrs (of manual setup removed (about 4 hours per rule))
The challenge:
Dealers on Nexgate publish their cars to multiple destinations. But some cars can't appear on some destinations. Manufacturer (OEM) rules can bring penalties if a car shows up in the wrong place.
The only safe option was to hold cars back entirely. About 20,000 listings were kept out of Nexgate for this reason. When a dealer did need a rule, it went from the dealer to our support team to engineering and back. Each rule took around 4 hours.
At the same time, Eagers, a group with 320 dealerships, was using a third-party publishing tool that cost them about $36K a year. We had a chance to win that business back, but only if dealers could manage rules themselves.
Key decisions:
- Map the logic before designing screens. I worked closely with the tech lead to map how the rules engine actually behaved, then co-designed the builder with Eagers using their real rules. This kept every design decision grounded in real cases rather than made-up examples.
- Everything publishes by default. Rules hold back the exceptions. We considered an approval-first model, where nothing publishes until a dealer allows it. I pushed for the opposite because it matched how the backend already worked and gave dealers a simple mental model: you only define what shouldn't go out.
- Test the engine before building the interface. Phase 1 was backend-only. We proved the rules engine worked before investing in a self-serve UI.
- Make it clear who's in charge. A manual publish always overrides automation, so dealers never feel the system is working against them. OEM rules are managed centrally because of the legal risk.
- Show the system's state. A rule can stop working if its destination becomes inactive. Rules show a clear state, either Active or Paused due to inactive destination, so dealers aren't left guessing.
- Solve the hardest case: Make → Model → Badge. Dealers needed rules like "restrict all Corollas except the ZR". I built four React prototypes to test this. I rejected locking the parent make, separate blocks per make, and full nesting, because each one either blocked real cases or became too complex. The final design keeps the cascade only for picking values and adds an "is not" option at each level. That handles the exception without flipping the whole rule.
- Keep the builder simple for non-technical users. I tested a sentence-builder and a wizard, and chose condition blocks. A fully nested AND/OR builder was left out of v1 because it tested badly with non-technical users.
The details that prevent mistakes:
Small decisions mattered because a wrong rule can hide stock or break OEM rules:
- Validation runs at the right moments: when a field loses focus, when a new condition is added, and on save. It doesn't nag while the dealer is still typing.
- Load failures have their own error state with Retry. If they fell through to the empty state, a dealer might recreate a rule that already exists.
- Closing with unsaved changes keeps the work. Only "Discard" clears it.
- The review screen mixes symbols and words. Symbols for comparisons (>, <), words for AND, OR and "is any of". This reads more clearly and works better with screen readers.
- Age units use visible chips instead of a hidden tap-to-cycle control.
Impact:
- Dealers using rules grew from 102 to 228 (+124%).
- 344 rules created, removing an estimated 1,300+ hours of manual setup (at about 4 hours per rule).
- Eagers is expanding rules to more dealerships, and the work is helping win them back from a competitor.
Quote: Eagers found it "super easy to use compared to the other solutions out there." (Relayed by our account team)
Reflection:
The hardest part of this project wasn't the interface. It was the logic underneath it. The backend didn't decide what to publish. It published everything and removed what matched a rule. I worked closely with the tech lead to understand that model before designing anything that would make sense to dealers.
The more I collaborated with engineering, the clearer the experience became. The backend model turned into the simplest rule for dealers: everything goes out, and you only define what shouldn't.
I mapped the rule logic and co-designed the builder with Eagers using their real rules, which kept the design grounded in real cases. What I'd do differently is test with a wider range of dealers. Eagers is a large group, and smaller dealers may set up rules in different ways.

CASE STUDY: AI Photo Enhancement. Designing trust, review and recovery around an AI feature, so dealers stay in control of what they publish.
Role: Product Designer, owned the gallery redesign, review and approval flow, and recovery model
Team: Product Manager, engineering (Web and iOS), other teams sharing the gallery, input from Eagers
Status: In build, pilot planned for mid-January 2027
Key numbers: 42 of 320 (Eagers dealerships use a professional photographer); 0 (AI-altered photos published without dealer approval); Web + iOS (one review and recovery model across both)
The challenge:
Photos sell cars, but dealer photos are inconsistent. Only 42 of Eagers' 320 dealerships use a professional photographer. Cars often sit dirty in holding yards, which slows how quickly they can be listed. Large dealer groups were starting to look at third-party AI tools.
AI enhancement could fix much of this: clean backgrounds, remove dirt and signage, improve lighting. But it brings a real risk. A car photo isn't just marketing. It's a record of the vehicle's condition. If AI hides a scratch or invents a detail, the dealer is responsible for that, not the AI.
The brief started as "dealer photos look bad". Before designing, I split it into three separate problems and moved missing angles into its own workstream (see Missing angles below).
So the design problem wasn't "how do we add an Enhance button". It was how to let dealers use AI with confidence, while making sure a person always checks and approves what gets published.
What dealers told us:
Early conversations with Eagers shaped the boundaries:
- They were comfortable with background replacement, removing dirt and signage, and better lighting.
- They were not comfortable with AI writing descriptions. One example was a model variant the AI suggested that was never sold in Australia.
- They were nervous that AI-generated angles could misrepresent a car's condition.
- They found labelling every edited photo cumbersome.
This set the line clearly: AI can improve how a photo looks, but never change what the car is.
Key decisions:
- Redesign the gallery, not just add to it. The gallery had an old look and feel and limited functionality. Instead of bolting enhancement onto it, I used the project to redesign it, so enhancement sits where dealers already manage photos. Other teams used the same gallery, so I worked with them to make sure the new version could be reused across their products too.
- Nothing AI-altered publishes automatically. Every enhanced photo needs dealer approval first. The original is always kept and can be restored, even after publishing.
- Change the flow from "preview and approve" to "inspect and confirm". A quick preview encourages a quick yes. I designed the review so dealers compare before and after with a slider (side-by-side as a fallback), review one photo at a time, and confirm the car's condition and features are accurate before approving. If they stop partway, a counter shows how many they've reviewed and approved.
- Use competitor research to set the review bar. Looking at how competitors handle uncertain results showed that CarCutter flags low-confidence outputs for a person to check. That shaped our review step: every enhanced photo is inspected by the dealer before it can be published.
- Give approvals their own space. Enhanced photos waiting for review go into an "awaiting approval" section. The gallery needs a save to update a listing, and processing is paid for, so an abandoned result shouldn't be lost.
- Treat recovery as part of version history. Once dealers could enhance, re-enhance and restore, these actions risked piling up in the gallery. I separated them into a clear model, so dealers know if they're creating a new version, going back to an earlier one, or returning to the original. Restore lives inside version history rather than as a separate gallery action, which also leaves room for more versions later.
- Make damage preservation non-negotiable. Keeping scratches and dents visible is always on. It isn't a dealer setting, because turning it off would undermine trust in every listing.
Missing angles:
Alongside enhancement, I designed how the gallery flags missing photo angles. Rather than copying a step-by-step guided walkthrough from another product, I recommended highlighting what's missing first, based on dealer skill level, photo volume and the yard environment.
The details that prevent mistakes:
- Errors are designed by recovery effort. If the dealer has to leave or replace a file, the message stays inline. If a quick retry fixes it, a toast is enough. Building this framework across Web and iOS also caught a real defect where the two platforms showed different causes for the same failure.
- Failures recover in bulk. A failed photo shows as a failed tile, and "Retry all" works the same way across photo, enhancement and 360 failures.
- Loading is honest. The AI gives no time estimate, so I used an ambient loading animation instead of a progress bar that would have been fake.
- 360 spin reuses the same model. Its button stays disabled until at least 4 angles exist.
- I tested and rejected a modal review, a persistent upload tray, and notifications for v1.
How I used AI:
I used Claude throughout this project, and checked everything it produced against our platform, our dealers and engineering.
- Challenging the brief. I asked Claude to push back on my framing before designing, which helped turn "dealer photos look bad" into three clear problems.
- Competitor research. Using live sources, it showed how competitors handle uncertain AI results, including CarCutter's low-confidence flags.
- Correcting a wrong assumption. In an early review flow, Claude assumed damage was marked as a region on the photo. Our system classifies the whole photo, so I corrected it with a screenshot of the real output. It had also left damaged photos out of enhancement. Engineering tests showed enhancement keeps damage visible, so I made those photos enhanceable and added a "check the damage is still visible" step to the review instead.
Claude got me to first drafts fast. The real output, the engineering tests and dealer input decided the design.
Impact:
The feature is in build, with a pilot planned for mid-January 2027. The design sets up a trust model where AI improves how photos look, while the dealer stays responsible for what's published, with a clear path back to the original at any point.
Reflection:
I used this project to redesign the gallery itself. It had an old look and feel and limited functionality, and other teams relied on it too. I worked with those teams so the new gallery could be reused across their products, not just for enhancement.
The hardest part was designing a lot of new functionality before we knew how the backend would support it. How to handle very large photo volumes, how dealers would be charged, and how to show AI confidence were all still open while I was designing. To keep moving, I worked through assumptions with engineering and designed flexible states that could adapt as those answers came in, rather than waiting for every question to be settled.
Two product decisions are also still open: whether to use one background across all of a car's photos, and whether to allow batch approval once single-photo approval is proven.

CASE STUDY: Inventory List. Bringing three pages into one, so dealers can spot, price and promote stock in one place.
Role: Product Designer, ran the cross-squad workshop, led layout concepts and dealer testing
Team: Product Manager, engineering, partner squads
Shipped: National rollout, November 2025
Key numbers: +55% (List page traffic (2.39M → 3.70M) as work moved into one page); 51 → 206 (leads a week from the Contact Watchers link, at peak); 36% → 61% (share of satisfied dealers, the month after rollout)
The challenge:
Dealers had three separate pages for managing stock: Inventory List, Promote and LiveMarket. Each had its own design and its own data. To spot a car that wasn't selling, reprice it and promote it, a dealer had to move across all three.
This was slow for dealers and expensive for us. The old system behind these pages was the most expensive one the company ran.
Process:
In May 2025, I ran a cross-squad workshop to bring the teams behind each page together. From there, I explored three layout concepts. Two went forward, a familiar "Default Page" and an "Insights View", and I tested them with 15 dealers.
Two findings shaped the direction:
- Dealers backed a single page over separate ones.
- Real-time market insights were unanimously the most valued addition, and the default view felt familiar enough to adopt without retraining.
Key decisions:
- One page instead of three. The concept test backed bringing list, pricing insight and promotion together. Dealers could now act on a car without leaving the page.
- Show signals, not just data. Each row shows market %, publishing age, stock age and health score together, so dealers can see which cars need attention at a glance. Sorting by last price change was added because dealers asked for it.
- Keep LiveMarket insight close, but clearly separate. LiveMarket data sits in the same row as the rest, so dealers see it in context. But it's visually set apart so it reads as extra insight, and it can be hidden for dealers who don't subscribe to LiveMarket. The same layout works for every dealer.
- Put buying where the decision happens. The Promote cart sits inside the list, so a dealer can promote a car the moment they decide to.
- Connect dealers with interested buyers. I designed a Contact Watchers link in the list, so dealers can reach buyers who are watching a car directly from the row.
- Don't show actions people can't complete. The Promote button is hidden for users without permission. Before, they could add to cart and then hit a wall at checkout.
- Cut what dealers don't use. Bulk edit was removed. Few people used it, and dealers whose stock is managed by their dealer system barely touched it. Single-item quick edit drew strong interest, so that's what we kept.
- Let the data set the default. The default sort changed from Make Z–A to Age (oldest first). 63% of users already chose age sorting, and Make Z–A wasn't in the top six. The data was clear enough that we made the change without extra research.
- Replace Print with Export, with a limit. Export is more useful for how dealers work today. The risk was that a full stock list could be shared with competitors, so export only includes the cars loaded on the page, not the whole inventory.
Impact:
- Activity moved into one page. List page traffic rose 55% (2.39M → 3.70M). Promote page traffic fell 36% and LiveMarket fell 19%, as expected, since that work now happens in the list.
- More leads for dealers. The Contact Watchers link lifted leads from 51 a week to 206 at peak (+303%), with 709 leads over 4 weeks, worth an estimated $47–57K.
- Satisfaction recovered after launch issues. Inventory CSAT dipped to 2.76 in November 2025, the rollout month, mostly because of bugs (around 70% of the dip). As we fixed them, it recovered to 3.61 the following month, and the share of satisfied dealers rose from 36% to 61%.
Quote: Dealers in concept testing unanimously named real-time market insights as the best addition. (Concept test findings)
Reflection:
The hardest part was making so much information scannable at a glance. I was combining three products, inventory, Promote and LiveMarket, into a single list, and every row had to stay easy to read.
LiveMarket was the trickiest. Its data had to sit in the same context as everything else, but be easy to recognise as extra insight, and be hidden for dealers who don't subscribe. Getting that balance right meant one design could serve every dealer.
What I'd do differently is push for a longer pilot before the national rollout. Around 70% of the satisfaction dip after launch came from bugs, and more time with a smaller group of dealers would likely have caught many of them first.

CASE STUDY: Inventory Details. Redesigning the vehicle details page so dealers can see what's happening with a car and act on it in one place.
Role: Product Designer: research (card sort and concept test), page layout, editing panel and status model
Team: Product Manager, engineering
Shipped: Pilot with 6 dealers in February 2026, national rollout March 2026
Key numbers: 29% → 61% (share of satisfied dealers in the two months after rollout); 8 dealers (card sort across cars, trucks, farm machinery, caravans and bikes); 50+ (dealer-system feeds kept working with the new status model)
The challenge:
A vehicle's details, its Promote products and its LiveMarket pricing all lived on separate pages. Dealers couldn't see cause and effect, such as whether a price change or a new photo actually led to more views and leads.
Editing was also scattered. Changing a listing sent dealers off to separate pages and back again.
We were also doing this with a much smaller team. Capacity fell 53% year on year (17 to 8 people), so every decision had to earn its place.
Research:
In mid-2025, I ran a card sort and a concept test with 8 dealers across cars, trucks, farm machinery, caravans and bikes. The card sort asked dealers what they actually needed on the page:
- Title, Health and Manage Publishing: 8 of 8 dealers
- LiveMarket pricing: 6 of 8
- Promote: 4 of 8
- Timeline: 2 of 8
This told us what to lead with and what could be smaller or conditional. It also showed that dealers split the page naturally into two kinds of information: what they need to know about a car, and what they need to do with it.
Key decisions:
- Context on the left, actions on the right. This came straight from the card sort. Health, Manage Publishing and Item History sit top-right, where dealers act. Vehicle information sits on the left, where they read.
- Show Promote only when it's relevant. Only half the dealers included Promote. Instead of giving it a large fixed section, it's simplified and appears when it's useful.
- Make health lead to a fix. Earlier, the health score just showed a number. Now each alert links straight to the field that needs fixing, so dealers can act right away.
- Edit in a side panel, not on separate pages. Editing opens in a wider, two-column, tabbed side panel. Dealers keep the context of the page and don't lose their place.
- Help dealers move around a long page. I added jump-to navigation as a dropdown in a thin strip between the tabs and the warning banner. I tested pills, a side rail and plain links, but they either took too much space or got lost.
The hardest decision: the status model:
A vehicle's status mixed two different ideas. "For Auction", for example, described both what kind of listing it was and how it would be sold. This caused confusing combinations. I worked through four options:
- Option A: Keep a flat list of values like "For Auction". Rejected because it kept the two ideas mixed up.
- Option B: Fully split into separate fields. Rejected because it would break more than 50 dealer-system feeds.
- Option C: Group the labels only. Rejected because it still left the stage unclear.
- Option D: Three fields (Listing Type, Stage, Purchase Type) behind an API that still accepts the old format. Chosen.
With Option D, the Stage a dealer picks controls which Purchase Types they can see. Invalid combinations can't happen, because the design prevents them rather than showing an error afterwards. And because the API still accepts the old format, none of the dealer-system feeds broke.
Impact:
- Fast rollout. The page went from a 6-dealer pilot to a national rollout in about five weeks.
- Satisfaction recovered after rollout. Inventory CSAT dipped to 2.41 in March 2026, the rollout month, then rose to 3.48 by May. Over the same two months, the share of satisfied dealers went from 29% to 61%.
- Positive pilot feedback. Pilot dealers described the page as cleaner and more modern, said updates went through faster, and found it easier to review a listing before it goes live.
- Follow-up releases. The Add/Edit panel and jump-to navigation have since shipped and have been well received so far.
Quote: "Looks much better than before and I am finding it easier to find prospect info now." (Dealer, inventory CSAT survey)
Reflection:
The hardest part was making the information on Inventory Details feel connected. Performance metrics showed what was happening, while the timeline recorded changes and activity. I wanted to bring those together so dealers could understand performance in the context of what had happened to the vehicle.
I learned that having useful information on the same page isn't enough. The relationships between it need to be clear. Connecting actions, timing and performance helps dealers interpret the numbers and make more informed decisions.
If I approached this again, I would explore those connections earlier and test them around specific dealer questions, such as "What changed after I adjusted the price?" This would help validate whether the page supports their reasoning and next steps.
