CASE STUDY: Inventory List (Inventory redesign · Part 1 · Discovery)
Bringing three pages into one, so dealers can spot, price and promote stock in one place.
What this shows: Discovery and product direction. Bringing three teams and three products together, then using dealer testing and usage data to decide what one page should do.
Role: Product Designer, ran the cross-squad workshop, led layout concepts and dealer testing
Team: Product Manager, engineering, partner squads
Shipped: National rollout, November 2025
Key numbers: +55% List page traffic (2.39M → 3.70M) as work moved into one page; 51 → 206 leads a week from the Contact Watchers link, at peak; 36% → 61% share of satisfied dealers, the month after rollout

Business challenge
Dealers had three separate pages for managing stock: Inventory List, Promote and LiveMarket. Each had its own design and its own data. To spot a car that wasn't selling, reprice it and promote it, a dealer had to move across all three.
This was slow for dealers and expensive for us. The old system behind these pages was the most expensive one the company ran.

Unknowns
Before designing one page, we had to answer a few questions:
- Would dealers actually prefer one page, or did separate pages suit how they worked?
- Which signals matter most when a dealer decides what to do with a car?
- Could one layout work for dealers with and without a LiveMarket subscription?

Investigation
In May 2025, I ran a cross-squad workshop to bring the teams behind each page together. From there, I explored three layout concepts. Two went forward, a familiar "Default Page" and an "Insights View", and I tested them with 15 dealers.
We also looked at how dealers already used the list. For example, 63% of users already sorted by age.

Key insights
- Dealers backed a single page over separate ones.
- Real-time market insights were unanimously the most valued addition.
- Familiar beats new. The default view felt familiar enough to adopt without retraining.
- Usage told us what to cut. Few people used bulk edit, while single-item quick edit drew strong interest.

Strategic opportunity
One page was a chance to do more than tidy up:
- Lower running cost. Moving off the old system behind these pages, the most expensive one the company ran.
- Insight next to action. With pricing insight and Promote in the same row, a dealer could act the moment they decided.
- A path to interested buyers. Showing watchers in the list, with a way to contact them, gave dealers a direct route to people already interested in a car.

Decisions and tradeoffs
Show signals, not just data. Each row shows market %, publishing age, stock age and health score together, so dealers can see which cars need attention at a glance. Sorting by last price change was added because dealers asked for it.
Keep LiveMarket close, but clearly separate. LiveMarket data sits in the same row, so dealers see it in context. It's visually set apart so it reads as extra insight, and it can be hidden for dealers who don't subscribe. The tradeoff was a busier row, but one layout works for every dealer.
Put buying where the decision happens. The Promote cart sits inside the list, so a dealer can promote a car the moment they decide to.
Don't show actions people can't complete. The Promote button is hidden for users without permission. Before, they could add to cart and then hit a wall at checkout.
Cut what dealers don't use. Bulk edit was removed. Dealers whose stock is managed by their dealer system barely touched it. Single-item quick edit is what we kept.
Let the data set the default. The default sort changed from Make Z–A to Age (oldest first). 63% of users already chose age sorting, and Make Z–A wasn't in the top six. The data was clear enough to make the change without extra research.
Replace Print with Export, with a limit. Export suits how dealers work today. The risk was a full stock list being shared with competitors, so export only includes the cars loaded on the page.

Solution
One list where each row shows a car's signals, its LiveMarket insight and the actions a dealer can take, including a Contact Watchers link I designed so dealers can reach watching buyers straight from the row.

Outcomes and learning
- Activity moved into one page. List page traffic rose 55% (2.39M → 3.70M). Promote page traffic fell 36% and LiveMarket fell 19%, as expected, since that work now happens in the list.
- More leads for dealers. The Contact Watchers link lifted leads from 51 a week to 206 at peak (+303%), with 709 leads over 4 weeks, worth an estimated $47–57K.
- Satisfaction recovered after launch issues. Inventory CSAT dipped to 2.76 in November 2025, the rollout month, mostly because of bugs (around 70% of the dip). As we fixed them, it recovered to 3.61 the following month, and the share of satisfied dealers rose from 36% to 61%.
Quote: Dealers in concept testing unanimously named real-time market insights as the best addition. (Concept test findings)
The hardest part was making so much information scannable. Every row had to stay easy to read while combining three products. LiveMarket was the trickiest: in context, easy to recognise as extra insight, and hidden for dealers who don't subscribe.
What I'd do differently is push for a longer pilot before the national rollout. Around 70% of the satisfaction dip came from bugs, and more time with a smaller group of dealers would likely have caught many of them first.


CASE STUDY: Inventory Details (Inventory redesign · Part 2 · Research)
Redesigning the vehicle details page so dealers can see what's happening with a car and act on it in one place.
What this shows: Research-led structure and complex data. Using a card sort to decide what the page leads with, and fixing a status model without breaking more than 50 integrations.
Role: Product Designer: research (card sort and concept test), page layout, editing panel and status model
Team: Product Manager, engineering
Shipped: Pilot with 6 dealers in February 2026, national rollout March 2026
Key numbers: 29% → 61% share of satisfied dealers in the two months after rollout; 8 dealers card sort across cars, trucks, farm machinery, caravans and bikes; 50+ dealer-system feeds kept working with the new status model

Business challenge
A vehicle's details, its Promote products and its LiveMarket pricing all lived on separate pages. Dealers couldn't see cause and effect, such as whether a price change or a new photo actually led to more views and leads.
Editing was also scattered. Changing a listing sent dealers off to separate pages and back again.
We were doing this with a much smaller team. Capacity fell 53% year on year (17 to 8 people), so every decision had to earn its place.

Unknowns
- What do dealers actually need on this page, and what can be smaller or only appear when it's relevant?
- How do we untangle vehicle status, which mixed two different ideas, without breaking the dealer-system feeds that depend on it?

Investigation
In mid-2025, I ran a card sort and a concept test with 8 dealers across cars, trucks, farm machinery, caravans and bikes. The card sort asked dealers what they actually needed on the page:
- Title, Health and Manage Publishing: 8 of 8 dealers
- LiveMarket pricing: 6 of 8
- Promote: 4 of 8
- Timeline: 2 of 8
For the status model, I worked through four options with the trade-offs of each (see Decisions below).

Key insights
- Dealers split the page into two kinds of information: what they need to know about a car, and what they need to do with it.
- Not everything deserves a fixed spot. Only half the dealers included Promote.
- A score isn't a next step. The health score showed a number, but didn't help dealers fix anything.
- Status mixed two ideas. "For Auction", for example, described both the kind of listing and how it would be sold, which caused confusing combinations.

Strategic opportunity
- Cause and effect in one place. Bringing details, Promote and LiveMarket together was a step towards dealers seeing whether a change led to more views and leads.
- Focus for a smaller team. The research showed what to lead with and what to simplify, which mattered with half the capacity.
- Protect the integrations. More than 50 dealer-system feeds depended on the status field, so fixing it safely protected those relationships.

Decisions and tradeoffs
Context on the left, actions on the right. This came straight from the card sort. Health, Manage Publishing and Item History sit top-right, where dealers act. Vehicle information sits on the left, where they read.
Show Promote only when it's relevant. Instead of a large fixed section, Promote is simplified and appears when it's useful.
Make health lead to a fix. Each health alert now links straight to the field that needs fixing.
Edit in a side panel, not on separate pages. Editing opens in a wider, two-column, tabbed side panel, so dealers keep their place on the page.
Help dealers move around a long page. I added jump-to navigation as a dropdown in a thin strip between the tabs and the warning banner. I tested pills, a side rail and plain links, but they either took too much space or got lost.
The hardest decision: the status model. I worked through four options:
Option A: Keep a flat list of values like "For Auction". Rejected because it kept the two ideas mixed up.
Option B: Fully split into separate fields. Rejected because it would break more than 50 dealer-system feeds.
Option C: Group the labels only. Rejected because it still left the stage unclear.
Option D: Three fields (Listing Type, Stage, Purchase Type) behind an API that still accepts the old format. Chosen.

Solution
A details page with context on the left and actions on the right, editing in a side panel, and a status model where the Stage a dealer picks controls which Purchase Types they can see. Invalid combinations can't happen, because the design prevents them rather than showing an error afterwards. Because the API still accepts the old format, none of the dealer-system feeds broke.

Outcomes and learning
- Fast rollout. The page went from a 6-dealer pilot to a national rollout in about five weeks.
- Satisfaction recovered after rollout. Inventory CSAT dipped to 2.41 in March 2026, the rollout month, then rose to 3.48 by May. Over the same two months, the share of satisfied dealers went from 29% to 61%.
- Positive pilot feedback. Pilot dealers described the page as cleaner and more modern, said updates went through faster, and found it easier to review a listing before it goes live.
- Follow-up releases. The Add/Edit panel and jump-to navigation have since shipped and have been well received so far.
Quote: "Looks much better than before and I am finding it easier to find prospect info now." (Dealer, inventory CSAT survey)
I learnt that having useful information on the same page isn't enough. The relationships between it need to be clear. Performance metrics showed what was happening, and the timeline recorded what had changed, but dealers need the two connected to understand the numbers.
Next time, I'd explore those connections earlier and test them around specific dealer questions, such as "What changed after I adjusted the price?"


CASE STUDY: Automation Rules (Distribution automation · Systems)
Turning a support-only process into a self-serve tool, so dealers control where their cars are published.
What this shows: Systems thinking and operational improvement. Understanding how a rules engine really behaves, then turning it into one simple model dealers can run themselves.
Role: Product Designer, owned the rule builder UX
Team: Product Manager, tech lead, engineering, co-designed with Eagers
Shipped: V2.0 national release, February 2026
Key numbers: 102 → 228 dealers using rules (+124%); 344 rules created; 1,300+ hrs of manual setup removed (about 4 hours per rule)

Business challenge
Dealers on Nexgate publish their cars to multiple destinations. But some cars can't appear on some destinations. Manufacturer (OEM) rules can bring penalties if a car shows up in the wrong place.
The only safe option was to hold cars back entirely. About 20,000 listings were kept out of Nexgate for this reason. When a dealer did need a rule, it went from the dealer to our support team to engineering and back. Each rule took around 4 hours.

Unknowns
- How did the rules engine actually decide what to publish, and could that logic be explained to dealers?
- Could non-technical dealer staff set up rules on their own, without support?
- How do we handle real exceptions, like restricting a model but allowing one badge, without making the builder too complex?

Investigation
- Mapped the logic first. I worked closely with the tech lead to map how the rules engine really behaved, before designing any screens.
- Used real rules. I co-designed the builder with Eagers using their actual rules, not made-up examples.
- Prototyped the hardest case. I built four React prototypes to test Make → Model → Badge exceptions.
- Compared formats. I tested a sentence builder, a wizard and condition blocks.

Key insights
- The backend already had the simplest model. It published everything and removed what matched a rule. So dealers only needed to define what shouldn't go out.
- Full nesting was too much. A nested AND/OR builder tested badly with non-technical users.
- Exceptions happen at every level. Dealers needed rules like "restrict all Corollas except the ZR".

Strategic opportunity
Self-serve rules could solve three problems at once:
- Hidden stock. About 20,000 listings were held out of Nexgate because dealers had no safe way to exclude some destinations.
- Support load. Every rule needed support and engineering, at around 4 hours each.
- Winning back a major customer. Eagers, a group with 320 dealerships, was paying about $36K a year for a third-party publishing tool. Self-serve rules gave us a way to win that business back.

Decisions and tradeoffs
Everything publishes by default. Rules hold back the exceptions. We considered an approval-first model, where nothing publishes until a dealer allows it. I pushed for the opposite because it matched how the backend already worked and gave dealers a simple mental model.
Test the engine before building the interface. Phase 1 was backend-only. We proved the rules engine worked before investing in a self-serve UI.
Make it clear who's in charge. A manual publish always overrides automation, so dealers never feel the system is working against them. OEM rules are managed centrally because of the legal risk, which means less dealer control there, on purpose.
Solve Make → Model → Badge without full nesting. I rejected locking the parent make, separate blocks per make, and full nesting, because each one either blocked real cases or became too complex. The final design keeps the cascade only for picking values and adds an "is not" option at each level.
Simple now, more power later. I chose condition blocks over a sentence builder and a wizard. The fully nested AND/OR builder was left out of v1, trading some flexibility for a builder non-technical users could actually use.

Solution
A rule builder made of condition blocks, with clear rule states and a review step before saving. A rule shows as Active, or Paused if its destination becomes inactive, so dealers aren't left guessing. Small details matter because a wrong rule can hide stock or break OEM rules:
- Validation runs at the right moments: when a field loses focus, when a new condition is added, and on save. It doesn't nag while the dealer is still typing.
- Load failures have their own error state with Retry. If they fell through to the empty state, a dealer might recreate a rule that already exists.
- Closing with unsaved changes keeps the work. Only "Discard" clears it.
- The review screen mixes symbols and words. Symbols for comparisons (&gt;, &lt;), words for AND, OR and "is any of". This reads more clearly and works better with screen readers.

Outcomes and learning
- Dealers using rules grew from 102 to 228 (+124%).
- 344 rules created, removing an estimated 1,300+ hours of manual setup (at about 4 hours per rule).
- Eagers is expanding rules to more dealerships, and the work is helping win them back from a competitor.
Quote: Eagers found it "super easy to use compared to the other solutions out there." (Relayed by our account team)
The hardest part wasn't the interface. It was the logic underneath. The more I worked with engineering, the clearer the experience became: the backend model turned into the simplest rule for dealers.
What I'd do differently is test with a wider range of dealers. Eagers is a large group, and smaller dealers may set up rules in different ways.


CASE STUDY: AI Photo Enhancement (Emerging tech · Responsible AI)
Designing trust, review and recovery around an AI feature, so dealers stay in control of what they publish.
What this shows: Emerging technology and trust. Deciding where AI should and shouldn't act, and designing review and recovery so a person stays responsible for what's published.
Role: Product Designer, owned the gallery redesign, review and approval flow, and recovery model
Team: Product Manager, engineering (Web and iOS), other teams sharing the gallery, input from Eagers
Status: In build, pilot planned for mid-January 2027
Key numbers: 42 of 320 Eagers dealerships use a professional photographer; Approval first design rule: no AI-altered photo publishes without dealer approval; Web + iOS one review and recovery model across both

Business challenge
Photos sell cars, but dealer photos are inconsistent. Only 42 of Eagers' 320 dealerships use a professional photographer. Cars often sit dirty in holding yards, which slows how quickly they can be listed. Large dealer groups were starting to look at third-party AI tools.
AI enhancement could fix much of this: clean backgrounds, remove dirt and signage, improve lighting. But a car photo isn't just marketing. It's a record of the vehicle's condition. If AI hides a scratch or invents a detail, the dealer is responsible for that, not the AI.

Unknowns
Much of this had to be designed before the answers existed:
- Where would dealers draw the line on what AI is allowed to change?
- How would the backend handle very large photo volumes?
- How would dealers be charged for processing?
- How should we show AI confidence?
Rather than wait, I worked through assumptions with engineering and designed flexible states that could adapt as answers came in.

Investigation
- Reframed the brief. It started as "dealer photos look bad". Before designing, I split it into three separate problems and moved missing angles into its own workstream.
- Talked to dealers. Early conversations with Eagers about what they would and wouldn't accept from AI.
- Looked at competitors. How other tools handle uncertain AI results.
- Checked with engineering. Engineering tests on whether enhancement keeps damage visible.

Key insights
- Dealers were comfortable with cosmetic changes: background replacement, removing dirt and signage, and better lighting.
- They didn't trust AI with facts. They didn't want AI writing descriptions. One example was a model variant the AI suggested that was never sold in Australia.
- Generated angles felt risky. Dealers were nervous AI-generated angles could misrepresent a car's condition.
- Labelling every edited photo felt cumbersome to them.
- Competitors keep a person in the loop. CarCutter flags low-confidence outputs for a person to check.
- Enhancement keeps damage visible. Engineering tests showed this, which changed how damaged photos could be handled.
Together these set a clear line: AI can improve how a photo looks, but never change what the car is.

Strategic opportunity
- Faster to list. Better photos without waiting for a clean car or a photographer.
- Keeping large groups on the platform. They were already looking at third-party AI tools.
- A better gallery for everyone. The gallery was old and other teams relied on it, so redesigning it could help their products too.

Decisions and tradeoffs
Redesign the gallery, not just add to it. Instead of bolting enhancement onto an old gallery, I used the project to redesign it, so enhancement sits where dealers already manage photos. I worked with the other teams that share it so the new version could be reused across their products.
Nothing AI-altered publishes automatically. Every enhanced photo needs dealer approval first. The original is always kept and can be restored, even after publishing. This adds a step for dealers, on purpose.
Change "preview and approve" to "inspect and confirm". A quick preview encourages a quick yes. Dealers compare before and after with a slider (side-by-side as a fallback), review one photo at a time, and confirm the car's condition and features are accurate before approving. A counter shows progress if they stop partway.
Give approvals their own space. Enhanced photos waiting for review go into an "awaiting approval" section. The gallery needs a save to update a listing, and processing is paid for, so an abandoned result shouldn't be lost.
Treat recovery as part of version history. Enhance, re-enhance and restore risked piling up in the gallery. I separated them into a clear model, so dealers know if they're creating a new version, going back to an earlier one, or returning to the original.
Make damage preservation non-negotiable. Keeping scratches and dents visible is always on. It isn't a dealer setting, because turning it off would undermine trust in every listing.
Highlight what's missing, instead of a guided walkthrough. For missing photo angles, I recommended highlighting what's missing first rather than copying a step-by-step walkthrough from another product, based on dealer skill level, photo volume and the yard environment.
Leave some ideas out of v1. I tested and rejected a modal review, a persistent upload tray and notifications for v1.

Solution
A redesigned gallery where enhancement, review, approval and recovery sit together, with one model across Web and iOS.
- Errors are designed by recovery effort. If the dealer has to leave or replace a file, the message stays inline. If a quick retry fixes it, a toast is enough. Building this across Web and iOS also caught a real defect where the two platforms showed different causes for the same failure.
- Failures recover in bulk. A failed photo shows as a failed tile, and "Retry all" works the same way across photo, enhancement and 360 failures.
- Loading is honest. The AI gives no time estimate, so I used an ambient loading animation instead of a fake progress bar.
- 360 spin reuses the same model. Its button stays disabled until at least 4 angles exist.

How I used AI
I used Claude throughout this project, and checked everything it produced against our platform, our dealers and engineering.
- Challenging the brief. I asked Claude to push back on my framing before designing, which helped turn "dealer photos look bad" into three clear problems.
- Competitor research. Using live sources, it showed how competitors handle uncertain AI results, including CarCutter's low-confidence flags.
- Correcting a wrong assumption. In an early review flow, Claude assumed damage was marked as a region on the photo. Our system classifies the whole photo, so I corrected it with a screenshot of the real output. It had also left damaged photos out of enhancement. Engineering tests showed enhancement keeps damage visible, so I made those photos enhanceable and added a "check the damage is still visible" step to the review instead.
Claude got me to first drafts fast. The real output, the engineering tests and dealer input decided the design.

Outcomes and learning
Measured outcomes: none yet. The feature is in build, with a pilot planned for mid-January 2027.
What the design sets up: a trust model where AI improves how photos look, the dealer stays responsible for what's published, and there's a clear path back to the original at any point.
Two product decisions are still open: whether to use one background across all of a car's photos, and whether to allow batch approval once single-photo approval is proven.
What I'm taking from this: you can design well before every answer is known, as long as the states are flexible and the assumptions are shared openly with engineering.
